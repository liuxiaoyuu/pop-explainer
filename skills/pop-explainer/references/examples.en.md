## Example 1: survivorship bias

**User:** explain survivorship bias like a story

🎬 The Story

Ming wanted to open a dumpling shop on the old high street. He walked the whole street and noticed that the three shops that had survived for thirty years all cooked over big wood-fired stoves.

"That's the secret!" He spent 20,000 building a wood-fired stove of his own (all numbers made up).

Three months in, customers hadn't increased, but his firewood bill had gone up 2,000 a month.

Old Xu, the cobbler next door, asked him one question: "Thirty years ago, how many dumpling shops were on this street?"

Ming had no idea. Xu counted on his fingers: at least twenty — and every one of them cooked with wood, because nobody had gas back then. One by one they closed, until only these three were left.

"The dozen-plus shops that closed had wood stoves too. You can only see the ones still open, so of course wood looks like the secret."

Ming stood there for a long time. The old shops cooked with wood not because wood kept them alive, but because everyone cooked with wood back then. What actually kept those three going — location, skill, cheap rent — he had never compared.

He took the photo of his stove off the wall. "I copied what every shop had, not what made these ones survive."

💡 This is called: Survivorship bias

When you only look at the winners still in front of you, you treat what they have in common as the secret of success — but the ones who disappeared may have had the same thing. Like the shops on that street: the ones that closed had wood stoves too; they just aren't there to see anymore.

⚠️ Don't Mix It Up

The useful comparison is between the ones that survived and the ones that didn't. Staring only at the survivors can't tell you anything.

## Example 2: Bayes' theorem (short form)

**User:** /simpler — I still don't get Bayes

🎬 The Story

A bike went missing from the apartment block. The camera only caught a blurry figure in a black cap and grey jacket. Leo, the new guard, slapped the desk: "That's Jay from Building 3 — he always dresses like that!"

The senior guard walked him to the gate and they watched for ten minutes. A dozen people in black caps and grey jackets walked by.

"Around thirty people here dress like that (all numbers made up). Only one of them took the bike."

Leo got it: the clue narrowed things down from hundreds of people to thirty — not to one.

💡 This is called: Bayes' theorem

New evidence should change your mind, but how much depends on how many other people also fit the evidence.

⚠️ Don't Mix It Up

It doesn't mean ignore evidence — it means common evidence points weakly.
